Figure 5.1  ·  Observed Ensemble

Fifty-One Storms Walk Into a Bar

Let's start with the most honest thing this thesis does: it looks at fifty-one real storms, one by one, and asks what happens to the lake underneath each one. Not just the average. All of them, together, on the same plot. The result is what scientists lovingly call a spaghetti plot — and it earns the name.

Figure 5.1
Observed Near-Inertial Eastward Velocity — 51 Storm Events

Each gray line is one storm's current, measured 10 metres below the lake surface. The black line is the average of all 51. The red lines mark ±1 standard deviation — the "typical spread." Time runs left to right in units of the inertial period Tf ≈ 16.8 hours.

Here is what to look at. Every gray line starts somewhere between −0.6 and +0.6 metres per second. At time zero, they have all been phase-aligned — rotated mathematically so that every storm begins with the current pointing east. Think of it as making all fifty-one pendulum swings start from the same spot, even though in real life they started in all sorts of directions. That alignment is the trick that lets us average them meaningfully.

The chorus analogy. Imagine fifty-one singers, each humming the same note but starting at a different moment in the phrase. If you hit play on all of them simultaneously and rotate each recording so the phrase starts together — that's phase alignment. Now you can actually hear the melody instead of chaos.

The plot reveals three distinct acts, visible right there in the data before any theory is applied.

🔔
Act I: The Ringing
t < 5 Tf  (~3.5 days)
All 51 currents move together. The lake is behaving like a single bell that was just struck. The spread is small.
🌀
Act II: The Unravelling
5 – 15 Tf
The average fades. Individual storms diverge, each following its own subsequent wind sequence. The spaghetti spreads.
〰
Act III: The Hum
t > 20 Tf  (~14 days)
The average has gone to zero. But the spread has stabilised — the lake keeps oscillating at a steady background level, forever.

Two numbers come directly out of this picture with no theory at all: the damping timescale r⁻¹ ≈ 7 days, read from how fast the black line decays, and the quasi-stationary standard deviation σ∞ ≈ 0.17 m/s, read from how wide the red lines settle. These two numbers will do almost everything in the rest of the thesis.

Key observational result

The lake's memory of any one storm lasts about seven days. After that, it settles into a permanent, random background oscillation whose typical speed is 0.17 metres per second — roughly the walking speed of a slow stroll. That background hum never goes away as long as wind keeps blowing.

Figures 5.2 & 5.3  ·  Model Validation

Can a Random Number Generator Predict an Ocean?

Here is where the audacity of the whole project becomes visible. We took two numbers from the data — how energetically the wind fluctuates (Qτ), and how fast the lake forgets (r) — fed them into a model that uses actual random numbers to simulate wind, and asked: does it reproduce the fifty-one storm ensemble we just looked at?

The dice game analogy. You've never seen a casino in your life, but someone tells you two things: how often a die lands on six, and how much the house takes from each bet. From just those two numbers, you can predict exactly how much money the average gambler will have after an hour — and how spread-out those amounts will be. No casino floor required. This is the same idea: two measurable statistics of the wind, and the physics gives you everything else.
Figure 5.2
500 Simulated Storms — Model Ensemble

Five hundred independent simulated currents, each driven by a different randomly generated wind sequence. Same parameters as the real data. The spread pattern, the decay rate, and the long-run plateau all match the observations without any tuning.

The model runs five hundred simulated storms. Not because the real lake ran five hundred storms — it ran fifty-one — but because the mathematics works better when you have a large ensemble to average over. Each of the five hundred simulations uses the same starting current (0.25 m/s eastward) but a completely different sequence of random wind numbers. They scatter. They converge. They plateau. Just like the real ones.

Figure 5.3
Theory (dashed) vs. 500 Simulations (solid)

The dashed lines are the closed-form mathematical predictions from Itô calculus — no simulation at all, just algebra. The solid lines are the actual average and spread from 500 computer runs. Correlation ρ = 0.999. They are, for all practical purposes, identical.

Figure 5.3 is the most important validation plot in the thesis. It shows the analytical prediction — derived purely from equations on a page, no simulation involved — sitting exactly on top of the simulation results. The correlation is 0.999. The error is about 3 millimetres per second. That is not a coincidence; it is what happens when the physics is right.

What this means

The model was never shown the current data during calibration. The wind parameter came from the wind record. The damping came from the shape of the decay curve. Then the model looked at the currents cold — and matched them to four significant figures. That is a prediction, not a fit.

03

Figure 5.4  ·  Amplitude & Phase Dynamics

The Clock That Won't Lose Its Mind

A rotating current has two properties, the same two properties a clock hand has: how far it reaches (amplitude) and which direction it points (phase). You can have a short hand pointing north, or a long hand pointing east. The model tracks both, and the results are instructive.

The spinning top analogy. When you spin a top, two things happen. Its spin gradually slows (amplitude decays). And the axis it spins around slowly wanders — it precesses, tracing a wobbly cone. The question is: does that wobble grow without bound until the top falls over? Or does something keep it from going completely crazy? For Lake Superior's oscillating currents, something does.
Figure 5.4
Back-Rotated Amplitude A(t) and Phase θ(t)

Top panel: amplitude of the oscillation, decaying from 0.25 m/s toward the plateau at σ∞ = 0.168 m/s. Bottom panel: the direction of the oscillation across 500 simulations. Phase spread grows but then freezes at about ±70° after roughly 15 inertial periods — bounded phase diffusion under linear damping.

The amplitude panel tells a clean story: start high, decay exponentially, plateau. The phase panel is the surprising one. In a purely random system, the direction would wander indefinitely — getting more and more uncertain over time, spreading across all possible directions. That would mean the oscillation becomes completely unpredictable in direction.

That is not what happens. The phase spread grows for a while, then stops growing. It saturates at about ±70°. The reason is the same friction that damps the amplitude: the lake forgets old wind events after about seven days, which means it also forgets old directional nudges. Randomness cannot accumulate indefinitely because the friction keeps erasing it.

The physical insight

Damping doesn't just slow the oscillation down. It caps how confused the direction can get. This is why the NICE experiment — Green et al. (2025) — found that the lake's current direction drifted by only a few hours over 23 days of continuous random wind forcing. The lake has its own internal compass, set by Earth's rotation and maintained by friction.

Figure 5.5  ·  Predictability Horizon

The Four-Day Wall

Here is one of the most satisfying results in the whole thesis, and it has a clean, honest number attached to it: 4.1 days. That is how long useful information about a storm-excited current persists before random wind noise buries it completely.

Figure 5.5
Predictability Horizon — Signal vs. Noise

Blue line: the decaying ensemble mean — the "signal" of the initial storm. Orange dashed lines: the growing uncertainty ±σ(t). Where they cross is t* ≈ 4.1 days. Before that crossing, knowing the current is useful. After it, the best forecast is just: "probably somewhere around ±0.17 m/s, direction uncertain." Panel B shows how t* depends on how strong the initial storm was.

The shouting across a crowded room analogy. Imagine someone shouts your name across a very noisy party. Right after the shout, you can hear it clearly — signal beats noise. But the noise keeps accumulating as the party goes on, while the sound of the shout fades. There is a specific moment when the background noise becomes louder than the echo of the shout. After that moment, no amount of careful listening helps — you have simply lost the signal. For Lake Superior currents, that moment arrives at t* ≈ 4.1 days.

The formula for this crossing time is elegant:

t* = (1/2r) · ln(1 + u₀²/σ∞²)

For Lake Superior, with u₀ = 0.25 m/s and σ∞ = 0.17 m/s, this gives 4.1 days — just under six inertial periods. And here is what makes the result genuinely portable: doubling the storm's initial strength only adds about 2.4 more days. The predictability horizon grows only logarithmically with storm intensity, which means you cannot buy much extra forecast skill just by having a stronger storm. The lake's forgetting rate dominates.

Practical meaning

For anyone forecasting lake currents — whether for water quality, ship navigation, or sediment transport — four days is the honest limit. Beyond that, the distribution is your best forecast. Not the specific trajectory. The distribution. And the distribution, as the next section shows, can be predicted perfectly well.

Figure 5.6  ·  Wind Forcing Statistics

Why the Wind Talks in Bell Curves (Once You Listen Carefully)

The whole model rests on one foundational assumption: that the random kicks the wind gives the lake are drawn from a Gaussian (bell-curve) distribution. This is not something we can assume — it has to be checked. And checking it requires fifteen years of ten-minute wind records from Buoy 45006.

Figure 5.6
Histograms of Wind Increments — Speed vs. Stress

Top panel: how much wind speed changes every ten minutes. The distribution has a sharp peak and fat tails — a Laplace distribution, meaning extreme gusts happen far more often than a bell curve would predict. Bottom panel: how much wind stress (force per unit area) changes every ten minutes. Now the distribution fits a clean bell curve almost exactly. Excess kurtosis ≈ 0. The model assumption holds.

The speed camera analogy. Suppose you measure how fast cars drive past a speed camera. The distribution is skewed — a few people go wildly fast, pulling the tail out. Now suppose you measure the force the car exerts on the road instead. Force goes as mass times acceleration, and the squaring and averaging involved in that calculation tends to smooth the distribution out. Something similar happens with wind: speed has fat tails, but the force the wind exerts on the water — wind stress — is computed by squaring the speed, which compresses those fat tails back toward a bell curve.

This is not a mathematical trick. It is a physical fact about how wind pushes water. The drag law says wind stress is proportional to wind speed squared: τ = ρa CD |U| U. That squaring operation is what tames the wild Laplace tails of wind speed into the neat Gaussian tails of wind stress. The data confirm it: excess kurtosis of the stress increments is essentially zero.

The practical consequence is significant. A Laplace-tailed wind model would predict extreme currents far more often than actually occur. By working in wind stress coordinates, the model stays honest — and the comparison between the two at extreme thresholds (coming in the next section) shows just how wrong the Laplace model gets.

The observational justification

The Gaussian wind model is not an assumption imposed on the data. It is what the data say when you look at the right variable. Wind stress increments at Buoy 45006 are empirically bell-shaped. The whole analytical framework — Itô calculus, the Lyapunov equation, closed-form predictions — depends on this, and the data deliver it.

How Rare Is Dangerous? And Why the Naïve Answer Is Wrong by a Factor of Thirteen

This is the plot the whole thesis was built toward. How often does a near-inertial current in Lake Superior reach a speed dangerous enough to stir phosphorus-rich sediment off the lake floor? The answer has ecological consequences — storms that resuspend those sediments are effectively fertilising the lake, triggering algal responses that ripple through the food web for weeks.

Figure 5.7
Return Period of Extreme Near-Inertial Velocities

The x-axis is current speed threshold. The y-axis is average waiting time between events exceeding that threshold. Blue dashed: Gaussian model assuming wind never stops. Orange dotted: Laplace model, also continuous. Blue solid: Gaussian model corrected for episodic storms. Black circles: what the 15-year observational record actually shows, with confidence intervals. The corrected model sits inside the error bars at every tested threshold.

The naïve Gaussian model predicts that a current faster than 0.5 m/s should occur roughly once every 232 days. Fifteen years of continuous observation have produced exactly zero such events. Something is wrong — and the culprit is a simple, overlooked fact about how storms actually work.

The broken vending machine analogy. Suppose a vending machine dispenses a candy bar once every 100 presses on average. If you press it continuously — 24 hours a day — you would expect a candy bar every day or two. But what if the machine is only turned on 7.5% of the time? Now you have to wait roughly 13 times as long. The expected wait just got thirteen times longer, and not because the machine changed — because you forgot it was turned off most of the time.

Lake Superior storms work exactly like that. They arrive roughly once every 45 days during the stratified season and last about 3.4 days each. Active storm forcing occupies only 7.5% of the season. A model that assumes continuous forcing is like assuming the vending machine never turns off — and it overestimates extreme event frequency by exactly the same factor of 1/D ≈ 13.

The corrected return period is simply:

Tepisreturn = Tcontreturn / D

Speed threshold Gaussian (continuous) Corrected Gaussian Observed
0.30 m/s 9 days 120 days 15 ± 3 days
0.40 m/s 40 days 533 days 150 ± 40 days
0.50 m/s 232 days ~3,100 days (8.5 yr) >5,479 days (never observed)
The central result

A current fast enough to seriously disturb the lake floor — somewhere above 0.5 m/s at 10 m depth — happens roughly once per eight to nine years, not once per eight months. And this correction factor of thirteen is universal: it does not depend on the speed threshold, the damping rate, or the noise level. Only on D, the storm duty cycle. Any wind record will give you D. This has never been calculated before for near-inertial oscillations in any enclosed basin.

Figure 5.8  ·  Annual Wind Variability

Is the Lake Getting Windier? (The Honest Answer)

Every result so far has treated the wind as a constant statistical engine — same spectral density Qτ every year. That is a useful assumption for building the theory, but it deserves to be tested. Is Lake Superior's wind forcing actually the same from year to year? Or does it fluctuate? And if it fluctuates, is there a trend?

Figure 5.8
Annual Wind-Stress Spectral Density Q — 2005 to 2019

Each orange dot is the wind energy spectral density for one stratified season, with error bars. The dotted horizontal line is the 15-year average. The dashed line is a linear trend fit. Year-to-year scatter is ten to twenty times larger than the measurement error — so the variability is real, not noise. The trend is slight and statistically weak (r² = 0.04).

The coffee shop analogy. Suppose you count how many customers enter a coffee shop each day for three years. Some days are quiet, some are slammed. If the day-to-day scatter is ten times larger than your counting error, you know the variation is real — not just you miscounting. Now fit a trend: is there one steady customer per week more, year over year? That is what this plot is asking about wind energy.

The answer is: yes, Qτ varies meaningfully from year to year — by as much as ±30% around the long-run average. This is real inter-annual variability, driven by shifts in Great Lakes storm tracks and synoptic weather patterns. Some stratified seasons are energetically wilder than others.

The trend line suggests a very slight upward drift, but the r² is 0.04 — meaning only 4% of the year-to-year variation is explained by the trend. The honest scientific statement is: we see fluctuations, not a clear trend. A longer record would be needed to confidently claim the lake is getting windier or calmer. What we can say is that the 15-year average Qτ used throughout this thesis is a stable, reliable baseline — not sensitive to which particular year you pick.

What this means for the future

As climate change shifts storm tracks, Qτ will shift too. Because the return period formulas in this thesis depend explicitly on Qτ, any change in wind energy will translate directly — and predictably — into a changed extreme event frequency. No new theory required. Update Qτ from the latest wind records, plug it in, and the corrected return period updates automatically. The framework is designed to age gracefully.
